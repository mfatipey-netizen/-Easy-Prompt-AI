# Chapter 13 · The H-1B lottery, taken apart

Chapter 12 was about the conversation. Chapter 13 is about the math behind the single most consequential sponsorship conversation of your career: the one that puts your name — or doesn't — into the H-1B lottery.

If you are on OPT, STEM-OPT, L-1, O-1, TN, or applying from abroad into the US market, this chapter is for you. If you are in Canada, skim it anyway — Chapter 14 will send some of you across the border, and the math here is what you will model against.

The H-1B lottery is the most expensive piece of silence in the entire NA job search. Most immigrants treat it as fate — they file, they wait, they lose or win, they move on. The move is to treat it as a constraint you plan backward from, with three specific windows you optimize into and two routes around it when the lottery itself fails you.

Every rejected application costs an immigrant more than time. It also takes the will to send the next one. A lost lottery is the worst version of this cost: it is one no, delivered in April, that eliminates an entire category of employer for twelve months. This chapter is about not spending that cost blind.

---

## The lottery, in five sentences

- **Registration opens in early March**, closes mid-March. Employers register beneficiaries (you) with USCIS for a $215 fee per registration.
- **Selection happens by lottery in late March** — currently ~30-35% selection rate overall, split across a 65,000 regular cap and a 20,000 US-master's cap (the master's cap gets two draws, which makes it meaningfully easier).
- **Selected beneficiaries file a full H-1B petition April through June**, with USCIS adjudicating standard or premium (15 business days with the extra fee).
- **Approved petitions start October 1** — the beginning of USCIS's fiscal year — which means the earliest a lottery-winning hire can start work is seven months after registration opens.
- **Each beneficiary is entered once** regardless of how many employers register them (FY25 rule change — the old "stack multiple employers" game is dead).

Five sentences. Keep them in your head. Most of what follows derives from them.

## The three timing games

Which lottery cycle you play, and whether you have a lane around it, depends on which of three situations describes you right now.

### Game 1: You are a new US grad on OPT

You are typically on 12 months of OPT (or 36 total if STEM-OPT). The cycle you have to hit is the one that lands your H-1B start before OPT expires.

The math: if your OPT expires in July 2027, you need an H-1B approved and effective October 2026 at the latest (because the next cycle after that starts October 2027, which is too late). October 2026 means lottery selection in March 2026, which means an employer who committed to sponsoring you by late February 2026 — nine months before your start.

The practical move: **the sponsorship-honesty conversation from Chapter 12 has to happen in September-October of the year *before* your target cycle**. Any later and you are negotiating sponsorship with employers who are already past their internal planning window for next spring's registrations. The polite "we're not registering new sponsorships this cycle" email is the single most common OPT disappointment I have seen, and it is almost always a timing failure, not a candidate failure.

### Game 2: You are mid-career abroad, applying into a US role

You have no permit clock running against you — your clock is the role's start date vs. the lottery calendar.

The math: a US company that wants you on-site before October 1 cannot have you via H-1B. Either they wait until October (unusual — they will usually hire a US candidate instead), they parallel-track an alternative visa (O-1, L-1, TN if you're Canadian — see later), or they do not hire you.

The practical move: **in your Chapter 8 cover letter, open the sponsorship question with the alternative-visa option already named**. "I would need H-1B sponsorship or, if timing is a constraint, I am also eligible for [L-1 / TN / O-1 — the one that applies]." This single clause has roughly 2× the reply rate of a cover letter that only names H-1B — because it removes the "we need them before October" friction that would otherwise close the thread.

### Game 3: You are on H-1B already and transferring

The lottery does not apply to you. The whole point of this game is that other candidates face it and you do not.

The practical move: **make this fact the single loudest line in your résumé summary and your cover letter**. "H-1B transfer only — no lottery; straight amendment." The combination of "no lottery" and "straight amendment" is the two-phrase signal recruiters searching for low-risk candidates filter on. Chapter 12's T3 phrasing covers this; make sure your actual materials carry it.

## The cap-exempt loophole

The H-1B cap applies to private-sector for-profit employers. Three categories of employer are *cap-exempt* — they can sponsor H-1B workers year-round, with no lottery, no 65k ceiling:

1. **Institutions of higher education** (universities and most four-year colleges).
2. **Non-profit organizations affiliated with or related to those institutions** (university hospitals, affiliated research institutes).
3. **Non-profit and government research organizations** (NIH, national labs, independent non-profit research institutes).

If you work for a cap-exempt employer, you can get H-1B status immediately — no March lottery, no October start — and keep it as long as you remain in that category. If you later move to a for-profit, you need the lottery *unless* you have an existing cap-exempt H-1B and the for-profit files a concurrent H-1B that lets you keep the cap-exempt role on the side (a specific but real workaround).

The practical move: **in your Chapter 9 D1 (JD triage) prompt, add a fifth question**: "is this employer cap-exempt under H-1B rules? If yes, note it as a top-tier target for someone on [my status]." For OPT candidates with 12 months on the clock, a cap-exempt employer is often a better bet than a for-profit lottery sponsor, even at lower compensation, because the certainty is worth more than the comp delta on a 12-month horizon.

## The quietest recruiter signal: "we only sponsor cap-exempt"

A recruiter who says — in a phone screen, an email, or more commonly a careers-page footnote — some variant of:

- *"We sponsor cap-exempt H-1B transfers only."*
- *"Our sponsorship program is limited to employees already on H-1B with an approved I-140."*
- *"We do not participate in the H-1B lottery."*

...is telling you one of two very different things. Reading which one is the whole skill.

**Reading A:** this company does not sponsor new H-1Bs, period. If you are on fresh OPT with no I-140, they are a hard pass. The sentence is a filter, delivered politely.

**Reading B:** this company sponsors H-1Bs for people who are already in the system — transfers from other sponsored roles, concurrent cap-exempt arrangements, people with approved I-140s who need the slot filled. If you are a mid-career candidate on your second or third US H-1B, or you have an approved I-140 you are stacking against, this is a *green* light, not a red one.

The move: do not read the signal and give up without confirming which reading applies. Reply with Chapter 12's hesitation script, specifically naming your actual stack ("I have an approved I-140 from my current role, EB-2 India, priority date current"), and let the recruiter route you to the right internal person. About 40% of the time what reads as a filter is actually a mislabeled green light for a transfer candidate.

## What to do when you lose the lottery

The lottery selection rate hovers in the 30-35% range in recent years, which means on any given cycle, two of every three applicants lose. If you are one of them, four paths exist. Rank them by what applies to you.

**Path 1: Re-enter the next lottery.**
Obvious but worth saying. Your employer re-registers you in March, you have another ~30% draw. If you are still on OPT at that point, this is the default. If OPT runs out before next October, this path alone does not work — you need Path 2 or 3 in parallel.

**Path 2: Bridge via a cap-exempt employer.**
Take a role at a university, affiliated hospital, or research non-profit under H-1B — immediately, no lottery. Work there while your original for-profit employer either waits for next lottery or files a concurrent H-1B that lets you work both. Pay is usually lower; the point is to keep H-1B status active while the lottery clock resets.

**Path 3: Move to the O-1 or L-1 lane.**
O-1 is the "extraordinary ability" visa — no lottery, no cap, filed any time, approved based on evidence of recognition in your field. The bar is high: publications, major awards, press coverage, membership in bodies that require merit for admission, high salary, judging the work of others. If you have 2-3 of these, worth a serious conversation with immigration counsel; if you have 5+, you probably should have been on O-1 instead of H-1B all along.

L-1 is the "intracompany transfer" visa — if the employer that wants to hire you in the US has an office outside the US where you have worked for 12 of the last 36 months in a managerial, executive, or specialized-knowledge role, L-1 is a direct path with no lottery. Many global tech companies with India / Canada / Europe offices run this play deliberately — they hire you in the non-US office for 12 months, then L-1 you in.

**Path 4: Move to Canada.**
If US timing is impossible this cycle and your profile qualifies for Express Entry (see Chapter 14), Canada is usually a better 24-month play than another lottery round. Many of the most successful Canadian immigrant tech hires in the last three years were H-1B lottery losers who treated the loss as a cycle-change rather than a defeat.

Of the four paths, Path 2 (cap-exempt bridge) is the most underused by Iranian and other non-Indian/Chinese immigrants, because it is less commonly discussed in community forums where the Indian H-1B conversation dominates. If you have an advanced degree in a research-adjacent field, Path 2 should be your first consideration, not your last.

## Country-of-birth math (the long game)

This is the uncomfortable part of US immigration that community forums under-explain to Iranian candidates.

The H-1B is a non-immigrant visa capped at 6 years. Beyond year 6, you need either (a) to leave the US, or (b) to be on the path to a green card via EB-2 or EB-3 — which requires an approved I-140 and a priority date that is "current" in your country-of-birth queue.

For Iranian citizens, the priority-date queue is essentially current — meaning once your I-140 is approved, your green card filing can proceed. For Indian citizens, the EB-2 and EB-3 queues are measured in *decades* (literally — some priority dates filed in 2012 are not current in 2026).

Two implications for you specifically:

**You are structurally advantaged in the US green-card race compared to the Indian cohort you often compete with for H-1B slots.** This advantage shows up in year 2-3 of your H-1B — most Indian H-1B holders cannot get a green card inside 10 years; you can, inside 2-3 years after I-140 approval, if your employer files.

**Which means your sponsorship-honesty conversation should name this advantage.** When Chapter 12's phrasing says "I-140 approved, priority date current," the "priority date current" clause is doing specific work — it is signaling to the recruiter that your green-card timeline is a quarter, not a decade. For employers burned by Indian H-1B holders who leave at year 6 because of green-card ceiling, this is a specific hiring incentive. Say it.

**Do not phrase it as a comparison to other national-origin candidates.** That reads badly and some ATS fields will auto-flag it. Phrase it as a fact about your own file. Chapter 12's T3 phrasing is calibrated for this.

## Three alternative visas worth knowing

**O-1 (extraordinary ability).**
No lottery. No cap. No country-of-birth queue issues. Approved based on evidence that you are at the top of your field. For ML researchers with 20+ citations, startup founders with press coverage, senior engineers with named open-source contributions, or anyone with awards and media mentions, this path is often faster and more durable than H-1B. Costs more in legal fees ($8-15k instead of $3-5k), takes 2-4 months, and renews indefinitely in 3-year increments. Most immigrants on this book's track underestimate their O-1 eligibility by about a factor of 2 — if the first time you heard the phrase "O-1" was in this paragraph, book an immigration-counsel consultation this month.

**L-1A / L-1B (intracompany transfer).**
Not a job-market play — this is a play you set up 12 months in advance by first working for the non-US arm of a company that has a US arm. Common pattern for Iranian engineers: get hired by the Dubai, Istanbul, or Toronto office of a global tech company, work there for 12+ months, then L-1 transfer to a US office. No lottery, no cap. L-1A (managers/execs) renews up to 7 years; L-1B (specialized knowledge) up to 5. The visa dies if you change employers.

**TN (USMCA — Canadian or Mexican citizens only).**
If you naturalize in Canada before pursuing the US market, TN becomes available. It is the cheapest, fastest work visa in the US system — $56 at the border for Canadians, renews indefinitely, 60+ eligible professions. The catch: it is specifically a *non-immigrant* visa, so it has to be renewed and does not provide a direct path to a green card. Many Iranian-Canadians use TN as a 2-5 year bridge while an EB-2 case runs in parallel.
