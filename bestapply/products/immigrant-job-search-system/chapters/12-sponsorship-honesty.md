# Chapter 12 · The sponsorship-honesty conversation

Part 4 is the immigration-specific chapters. The toolkit from Part 3 works for every job seeker; Part 4 is where it gets *sharpened* for people on a permit — because the recruiter on the other end of your application is doing a different math on you than on a citizen, and that math is where most immigrants lose before the race starts.

Chapter 12 is first because this one conversation — nine words, delivered at the right moment, in the right phrasing for your lane — changes who calls you back. Most immigrants never have it. They let the recruiter raise it, let the awkwardness compound, and then watch the thread go silent.

The move is to say it first. On your terms. In nine words.

Every rejected application costs an immigrant more than time. It also takes the will to send the next one. This chapter is where you stop paying the sponsorship tax by surprise and start pricing it in.

---

## What is actually happening on the other side of the screen

Every recruiter who sees your résumé is doing one of two silent calculations:

- **If you look like a citizen or PR / Green Card holder:** they move you to the next step based on fit alone.
- **If you look like a permit holder:** they add roughly 30-45 seconds of mental cost — "do we sponsor? who signs off? is this hire's start date before or after the lottery? did we burn our quota last year? is the hiring manager going to push back?"

That 30-45 seconds is where most immigrant applications die. Not because recruiters are hostile — most are not — but because 30-45 seconds of unresolved risk is 30-45 seconds too many on a candidate they have never spoken to. The résumé goes to the "maybe pile," which is the "no pile" with better lighting.

The sponsorship-honesty conversation collapses that 30-45 seconds to zero. You answer the question before they ask it, in a form they can act on without escalating.

That is the whole mechanism. The rest of this chapter is the *how*.

---

## The nine words

The core sentence, in neutral form:

> **"My work authorization is [status], no sponsorship required."**

Nine words. Noun, verb, status, close. No apologies, no explanations, no compound clauses. If "sponsorship required" applies to you — and the following sections cover all the variants — you adapt it exactly, not more:

> **"My work authorization is [status], and I will need [sponsorship type]."**

Why this works, and why a longer sentence does not:

1. **It names the status in the applicant's own words.** The recruiter does not have to guess, map, or escalate to legal.
2. **It resolves the sponsorship question in the same breath.** No "we'll have to come back to that" moment on a first call.
3. **It is short enough to copy-paste.** Recruiters forward one-line facts to hiring managers; they summarize paragraphs.
4. **It uses the recruiter's vocabulary.** "Work authorization" and "sponsorship" are the two terms every NA ATS and recruiter database tags on. Using them exactly places you in the right bucket on the first pass.

The three failure modes immigrants fall into with this sentence:

- **Over-explaining.** "I came to Canada in 2024, finished my master's at UofT, got my PGWP in June, which is valid for three years, and I don't need sponsorship for this role but might in the future." Nineteen words too many. The recruiter stops reading at word 10.
- **Hedging.** "I believe I should be eligible to work without sponsorship, pending final paperwork." Translates to "do not trust the start date."
- **Burying it.** Placing the sentence at the bottom of the cover letter or in a résumé footer. The recruiter stops reading above it; the ATS deprioritizes footer content (see Chapter 7).

Sentence at the top. Nine words. Then move on to what you actually do.

## The six phrasings — four permit lanes

The core sentence has one slot: your status. Fill it with the exact phrasing for your lane. The phrasings below pass clean through every NA ATS field validator and read natural to a human recruiter.

### Canada — PGWP

> *"My work authorization is a Post-Graduation Work Permit valid through [MM/YYYY], no sponsorship required."*

Why "valid through" and not "valid for 3 years": recruiters calculate against calendar dates, not durations. "Valid through 03/2028" is immediately actionable; "valid for 3 years" requires them to look up your issue date.

### Canada — Bridging Open Work Permit (BOWP) or PR-in-progress

> *"My work authorization is a Bridging Open Work Permit while my PR application is in final processing, no sponsorship required."*

The BOWP is often misread by US-based recruiters serving Canadian clients. The phrase "while my PR application is in final processing" is the one sentence that stops the misreading — it signals that your status is strengthening, not weakening, which flips the recruiter's risk calculation.

### Canada — Permanent Resident

> *"I am a Canadian permanent resident, no sponsorship required."*

Shortest of all. For PR holders the only mistake is adding more — "landed in 2024, PR card renewed, …" is noise. "Canadian permanent resident" is the recruiter's hard-match keyword; everything after it dilutes.

### US — H-1B transfer (approved I-140, current priority date)

> *"I am on an H-1B with an approved I-140 and a current [country of birth] priority date — transfer would be a straight amendment."*

This is the only phrasing in the book that goes above nine words, and it is worth every extra word. "Straight amendment" is the term recruiters and immigration counsel look for to confirm that a transfer is procedural, not lottery-dependent. Without it, you are treated as a lottery candidate. With it, you are treated as a current-cycle hire.

### US — OPT / STEM-OPT

> *"I am on [OPT / STEM-OPT] valid through [MM/YYYY]; H-1B sponsorship would be needed at that point."*

The sentence that most F-1 students get wrong. The failure mode is leaving sponsorship ambiguous — "I may need sponsorship in the future." Recruiters hear "may" as "will complicate my Q4." Say exactly when, and name the type (H-1B specifically, not "a visa").

### US — Green Card holder or EAD

> *"I am a US permanent resident (Green Card holder), no sponsorship required."* **or**
> *"I hold an EAD valid through [MM/YYYY] under [AOS / asylum / other category], no sponsorship required for this role."*

For EAD holders, the category matters because the recruiter's internal system has different approval paths. "EAD under AOS" (adjustment-of-status) is read as PR-adjacent and is low-friction; "EAD under asylum" is read as needs-renewal-tracking and sometimes routes to legal. Both are fine; both work; naming the category up-front controls the routing.

### US — TN visa (bonus)

> *"I am a Canadian [or Mexican] citizen eligible for TN status under USMCA; no annual lottery applies."*

The TN is the easiest sponsorship in the US system and the one recruiters most often forget exists. "No annual lottery applies" is the sentence that lands with any recruiter burned by the H-1B lottery once.

## When to initiate

Three triggers mean *you* raise the sponsorship question first, before the recruiter does. Not raising it is costing you replies.

**Trigger 1: before the first cover letter in a new company.**
Put the nine-word sentence in paragraph 1 of your cover letter. Chapter 8's twenty letters all do this. If yours does not, every recruiter at every target company spends their 30-45 seconds on it before reading paragraph 2 — and most never reach paragraph 2.

**Trigger 2: within the first 90 seconds of a phone screen.**
After the small-talk opener, after the recruiter asks "walk me through your background" and you give the 60-second answer, close that answer with the nine-word sentence. Not at the end of the call. Not when they ask about availability. In the first 90 seconds, in your own narrative, where it reads as confidence, not as a flag.

**Trigger 3: in the first LinkedIn DM to any recruiter.**
Chapter 9's prompt D6 bakes this in — the three-sentence outreach formula ends with the authorization line. If you built your DM template without it, the recruiter has to click into your profile to figure it out, and half of them do not.

Three triggers. One sentence. If you cannot hit all three habitually, you are leaking replies in a measurable way.

## When NOT to initiate

Two moments where volunteering sponsorship information too early *hurts* you. Both are easy to misread.

**Not in the first paragraph of a 2nd-degree LinkedIn message to a hiring manager (not a recruiter).**
Hiring managers do not want to be the person processing your sponsorship question — they want to want to interview you, and let a recruiter sort out logistics later. If you lead with status in a direct hiring-manager DM, you have made yourself their administrative problem. Lead with the specific technical overlap; let them refer you internally; let the recruiter run the authorization conversation.

**Not in the first email of a warm referral introduction.**
When a friend-of-a-friend introduces you to someone at a target company, the introduction email is where you demonstrate what you *do*, not what you *are*. Save the sponsorship line for the first scheduled call, where it fits inside your 60-second background narrative. Opening a referral intro with authorization reads as a candidate who thinks their status is the headline — exactly the opposite of the impression a warm intro is supposed to create.

Everywhere else: lead with it.

## What to do when the recruiter responds

The nine-word sentence does three things to the recruiter's response pattern. Train yourself to read them.

**Response pattern 1: relief.**
*"Oh, perfect — that makes this much easier."* or similar. This is the response you want. The recruiter was pre-emptively worried, your sentence resolved it, and now the rest of the conversation is about fit. No follow-up needed except to carry on.

**Response pattern 2: hesitation.**
*"Let me check with our team about [sponsorship type]."* or *"We've had some complications with [permit type] in the past."* This is the response that reveals you are talking to a recruiter whose company has a specific policy constraint. Do not try to argue. Respond:

> *"Totally understood. If it would help, I'm happy to send you a one-page summary of my status with the specific document references — most immigration counsel I've worked with can confirm in a 20-minute call. What is the typical process on your end?"*

That reply hands them control, offers them documentation, and asks them to name the process. One of three things happens next: they come back with a concrete process (good), they come back with "we don't sponsor this type at this time" (also good — you now know, and did not lose weeks finding out), or they ghost (also informative, if less pleasant).

**Response pattern 3: pivot.**
*"We're not sponsoring this role, but we have [other role] open where authorization isn't a question — would that interest you?"* This happens more than people expect, and it is almost always a legitimate offer. If the other role is within one step of your target, say yes to the conversation. You lose nothing by having it.

## The two-sentence version for recruiter database fields

Portal application forms and recruiter CRM systems sometimes have a dedicated "work authorization" free-text field (Workday, Lever, and Ashby all do). The nine-word sentence is too short for a field designed to capture nuance. Expand to two sentences:

> *"My work authorization is [status], valid [through MM/YYYY / permanent]. No sponsorship is required for this role."*

The second sentence closes the loop on this specific role, which is what the field is actually asking. Add a third sentence only if the sponsorship picture changes mid-role (e.g., OPT → H-1B):

> *"My work authorization is [status], valid through [MM/YYYY]. No sponsorship is required to start. H-1B sponsorship would be needed at that renewal date."*

Three sentences, maximum. Any more and the field reads as evasive.
