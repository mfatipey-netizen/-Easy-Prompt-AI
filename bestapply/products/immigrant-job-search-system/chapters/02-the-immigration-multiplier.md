# Chapter 2 · The immigration multiplier

Ask any Toronto immigration lawyer what a 90-day gap in status costs a family, and you'll get a number between C$8,000 and C$25,000. That is not the lawyer's fee. That is the total: bridging fees, medicals redone, RCMP checks redone, unlawful-work penalties dodged, the flight your parents booked to visit that you can't take because your PR file went cold, the deposit on a rental you couldn't sign because your permit expires in 60 days.

That number is invisible in your bank account. Until it isn't.

Your citizen colleague at the next desk, doing the exact same job hunt, faces none of it.

This is the chapter that puts a dollar sign next to the sentence that runs through the whole book:

> **Every rejected application costs an immigrant more than time. It also takes the will to send the next one.**

Time we can now name. And once we name it, the entire strategy of the book changes shape.

---

## The 3× rule

Take a citizen applying to jobs. Their per-application cost is:

- 45 minutes of research + writing
- Maybe C$5 in coffee
- A little emotional friction on rejection day

Take you. Your per-application cost is the same 45 minutes plus a share of six invisible line items:

1. **Runway.** Every application you send that fails is a slice of your permit that could have gone to a better-fitted one.
2. **Sponsor-filter risk.** Some percentage of the jobs you apply to don't sponsor visas — and you may not know which. Chapter 4 will fix this. The cost lives here.
3. **Legal-fee accumulation.** Every six months without a stable job, your lawyer adds a new advisory line to your bill.
4. **PR-points erosion.** Every month without Canadian professional experience is CRS points bleeding out of your Express Entry profile.
5. **Family standing.** The parents-in-law now ask, every phone call. Your spouse's Open Work Permit renewal is coming.
6. **Recovery cost.** Rejection makes you slower on the next application. You take an extra 15 minutes just to open the browser.

Sum it in dollars. Sum it in weeks. It comes out to roughly three times the citizen's cost — a "3× rule" that the immigration lawyers I interviewed for this book called *conservative*.

Three times the pain, same 2% success rate.

If you were building a startup with that unit economics, you'd shut it down. Job seeking is the one product we don't apply that logic to. This book is a way to.

## The sponsor tax: how to read a posting in 15 seconds

Some jobs never wanted you. The posting looks clean, the job looks perfect, and your résumé matches the keywords cleanly. What you can't see is a filter that runs *before* the ATS: whether the company sponsors visas at all.

In Canada, most private-sector jobs above C$100k technically *can* sponsor (through LMIA-exempt paths or Provincial Nominee Programs), but many don't want the paperwork. In the US, sponsorship is a much bigger commitment — the company has to lock in $10k+ in H-1B legal fees plus the risk of the lottery.

Here is a 15-second read on any posting.

**Green flags** — this employer will likely sponsor:

- "Work authorization in [country] required" — no mention of "current" or "no sponsorship offered"
- "H-1B transfers welcome" — US, biggest signal
- "Open to candidates on PGWP / BOWP / Express Entry" — Canada
- Company is publicly listed and has hired H-1B or LMIA candidates in the last 2 years (searchable on the public H-1B database or the LMIA public list)
- Bank, top-tier consulting, big tech, big pharma — historically high sponsor rate

**Yellow flags** — maybe, ask before applying:

- "Must be legally authorized to work" with no mention of sponsorship policy
- Small (< 50 employees) private company with no immigration history
- Government contractor posting — many US federal contracts require citizenship

**Red flags** — do not spend 45 minutes on this:

- "Must be a permanent resident or citizen." This is not a keyword filter — it is a legal filter for a defense, cleared, or regulated role. Don't waste the runway.
- "No sponsorship provided now or in the future."
- Job is at a US federal agency, defense contractor, or cleared-only role.
- Job requires TS/SCI or a similar clearance you cannot obtain.

The 15-second read saves you from what I call the sponsor tax — applying to postings that were never going to consider you. Immigrants who never do this filter lose 15–25% of their application budget to sponsor-tax rejections. Chapter 4 will build the tooling; this chapter is about seeing the pattern.

## The runway equation

Now the math you actually came here for.

Let:

- **R** = months remaining on your current permit
- **A** = applications you can realistically send per week — a *sustainable* pace, not a burnout sprint. Usually 12–20 for one hour a day.
- **P** = your genuine response rate. For immigrants sending keyword-optimized applications, this typically runs **4–7%**, not the 2% median — because you self-filter to well-matched postings.

Your quality-application budget before permit urgency:

    Budget = R × 4 × A

Your expected responses:

    Responses = Budget × P

Your expected offers, cumulative (response → first-round ~40%, first-round → onsite ~30%, onsite → offer ~50%):

    Offers ≈ Responses × 0.4 × 0.3 × 0.5
             ≈ Responses × 0.06

If R = 12, A = 15, P = 0.05:

    Budget    = 12 × 4 × 15 = 720 applications
    Responses = 720 × 0.05  = 36
    Offers    ≈ 36 × 0.06   = 2.16

Two offers in a year, on 720 quality applications. Enough to negotiate. Enough to breathe.

If R = 4 instead of 12, the math turns brutal. You have room for ~240 applications, ~12 responses, ~0.7 offers. Below one expected offer. This is the moment "spray and pray" becomes even more mathematically absurd — because your only lever is quality, not quantity.

The worksheet lives in the appendix as `runway-worksheet.pdf`. Fill in your actual R, A, P today. It takes ninety seconds and it will change what you do this weekend.

## Three concrete moves

**1. Never spend 45 minutes on a posting until you've done the 15-second sponsor read.** Not applying is fine. Applying to a job you cannot legally take is a total loss.

**2. Track your response rate, not your application count.** Applications are inputs; responses are outputs. Responses per week is the number that tells you whether you're getting closer. Chapter 8 shows you how to raise it 3–5× on the same time budget.

**3. Pick a permit-urgency lane.** If R > 8 months, you can afford to swing at slightly off-pitch postings. If R < 4 months, every application must be signal-optimized. There is no middle ground; work out which lane you're in this week.
