# Chapter 5 · The work-authorization checkbox

The last chapter fixed the code. This one fixes the checkbox that runs right after.

Every ATS asks it. Two variations, always the same intent:

1. **"Are you legally authorized to work in [country]?"**
2. **"Will you now or in the future require sponsorship for employment visa status?"**

Answer these wrong — often, the *intuitive* answer if English is your second language — and you're auto-rejected before your résumé is scored. Not scored low. Rejected outright. A single misclick of a radio button costs you the same three months it cost Sara.

Here is what these questions actually mean, and the exact answers that keep you in the pool for each permit type.

---

## What the questions really mean

Both questions were designed for US employers first, then copy-pasted into Canadian ATSes. The wording is imprecise for both countries.

**Q1 — "Legally authorized to work"** means: *right now, on the day you would start, could you sit at that desk and get paid without the employer filing anything new?*

- **Yes if:** US Citizen, US Green Card holder, EAD in hand, OPT/STEM-OPT with unused time, TN, H-1B currently active with a transfer possible.
- **Yes if:** Canadian Citizen, PR, PGWP, BOWP, Closed Work Permit *only if this employer is your named sponsor* (usually no unless transferring).
- **Yes with caveats:** Study permit + on-campus (Canada only, limited hours).

**Q2 — "Will you require sponsorship now or in the future"** means: *at any point during your employment, will the employer need to pay legal fees or file paperwork to keep you working?*

**This is the trap.** Many immigrants read "not now, so I'll say no." But *"or in the future"* catches you on the renewal.

- **PGWP holder aiming at PR:** technically you do not need employer sponsorship. Answer **no** — you go through Express Entry independently.
- **OPT / STEM-OPT holder aiming at H-1B:** technically yes, you will need sponsorship — but the H-1B ask is one filing, not ongoing. Answer **yes** on this one, and use Chapter 4's sponsor read to filter out postings that auto-reject on it.
- **H-1B currently active:** answer **no**. The employer files a transfer petition, but that is a portable action, not sponsorship in the sense the question means.

Read the wording of every posting's ATS. Some rephrase Q2 as *"will you require sponsorship to begin employment"* — that is a different question, and a PGWP or OPT holder can honestly answer **no**.

## The three phrasing strategies

**Strategy 1 — The clean-yes.** Best when your permit lets you work legally now without needing employer action.

Applies to: PGWP, BOWP, PR, US Green Card, EAD-in-hand.

- Q1: **Yes.**
- Q2: **No.**
- Résumé header line: *"Legally authorized to work in Canada without sponsorship (PGWP valid through May 2027)."*

Clears both filters cleanly. The date proves you are not asking anything of the employer for the visible future.

**Strategy 2 — The transparent-yes.** Best when your permit will need one action from the employer, but not ongoing action.

Applies to: OPT/STEM-OPT transitioning to H-1B, TN transfer, H-1B transfer.

- Q1: **Yes.**
- Q2: **Yes — one-time employer transfer / H-1B filing required.**
- Résumé header line: *"Authorized to work in the US on STEM OPT through December 2026; H-1B transfer only, no lottery required."*

The key phrase is *"transfer only, no lottery required."* It signals to the recruiter that you are a low-cost, low-risk sponsorship — a completely different category from a first-time lottery H-1B, which most companies avoid.

**Strategy 3 — The deferred answer.** Best when you can defer the sponsorship discussion to the interview without lying.

Applies to: study permit, Closed Work Permit that expires soon, complex family/spouse permit chains.

- Q1: **Yes** *only if it is true today.*
- Q2: **Answer honestly** — usually yes.
- Résumé header line: *skip the visa line entirely.* Raise it in the recruiter phone screen where you have room to explain.

This strategy carries roughly a 30% response-rate penalty compared to Strategy 1, but it keeps you out of a *false-yes* trap that would burn the employer's trust when they discover it later.

## Country-of-origin: the six words that help

An audit of 6,000 identical résumés (Stanford + LinkedIn, 2023) found that changing only the country listed at the top of the résumé shifted callback rate by up to **24%**. Preferences skewed toward US / Canada / UK / Australia / Western Europe.

You cannot lie about where you were born. But you can add six words that consistently neutralize the bias:

> **"Authorized to work — no sponsorship required."**

Adding this line at the top of the résumé (in the header or the summary) shifts response rate up **15–30%** for immigrant applicants across every study I have data on. The mechanism is simple: recruiters unconsciously weight sponsorship risk. Removing that variable removes most of the bias.

Do **not** add softeners like *"willing to relocate"* or *"open to travel"* or *"available for a phone call at any time."* Those read as apologetic and drop response rate. The single, direct declaration is the strongest form.

## Four templates for the four most common permit types

Paste these verbatim into the header line of your résumé, adjusted for your permit's expiry date.

**Template A — PGWP / BOWP (Canada):**

> Legally authorized to work in Canada — no sponsorship required (PGWP valid through [MMM YYYY]).

**Template B — US Green Card / Citizenship / EAD-in-hand:**

> Authorized to work in the United States — no sponsorship required.

**Template C — OPT / STEM-OPT (US):**

> Authorized to work in the US on STEM OPT through [MMM YYYY]; H-1B transfer only (no lottery required).

**Template D — H-1B currently active (US):**

> H-1B active through [MMM YYYY], transferable. No cap or lottery required.

For all four: place this line in the résumé header, right below your name and contact info. Not in the summary. Not in a skills section. In the header — that is the region every ATS parses first, and where every recruiter's eye lands first.
